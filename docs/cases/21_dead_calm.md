---

La Sierpe is a brigantine of one hundred and forty tons, eighteen guns, and thirty-one souls. She has been operating under a Letter of Marque issued by a Jamaican governor whose relationship with the Crown is best described as flexible. The letter authorises her to interdict Spanish shipping in the waters between Hispaniola and the Windward Passage. It does not authorise several of the things she has done in the past eighteen months, but the governor's flexibility extends to interpretation as well as issuance.

Her captain is a woman named Maren Solís — no relation to the conservatory tutor, though both carry the same quality of compressed fury beneath a professional surface. She has commanded La Sierpe for three years, inherited the captaincy from her predecessor in a manner the crew found entirely convincing, and has run a tight, profitable, and surprisingly democratic operation by the standards of the Caribbean in 1698.

On the fourth day of a dead calm, her quartermaster is found floating face-down off the starboard bow at first light. His name is Tobias Crane. He has a single wound under his left arm, between the ribs, made by something narrow and long. He did not fall. He was put there.

---

## The Dead Calm

Four days without wind means four days without movement, without purpose, without the ship's ordinary rhythms that give thirty-one people structure and distance from each other. Two men have already fought over shade. The food stores are being assessed.

It also means no one has left the ship. No longboat has gone out. No contact with any other vessel. The killer is one of thirty-one people on a floating prison and has been within shouting distance of every other suspect since the wind died. The field is closed. Every token belongs here. The truth has been here all along.

---

## Crane

Tobias Crane is forty-four and has been at sea since he was twelve. He has been La Sierpe's quartermaster for six years — the crew's elected representative, the counterbalance to the captain's authority. He has played this role with a pragmatism that made him trusted by both sides.

He was also, for the past four months, keeping a second set of records.

Not the ship's log. A private ledger, written in a cipher of his own devising, documenting the true accounting of La Sierpe's operations — the prizes taken, the cargo values, the distributions, and the consistent discrepancy between what the crew received and what the actual prize values indicated they should have received. Someone has been skimming. The ledger names the amount. It does not yet name the person. Crane was still working on that.

---

## Maren

Captain Maren Solís is thirty-seven. She is not the killer. She is the person who has been skimming.

She has been taking a larger share than the ship's articles entitle her to for eighteen months — not dramatically, consistently — because she has been buying intelligence from a contact in Havana about Spanish convoy schedules, paying for it from the prize pool rather than her own share, because the contact's fees have been increasing and she cannot explain the expenditure without explaining the contact, and the contact is the source of La Sierpe's exceptional success. She has been robbing her crew to pay for the intelligence that makes her crew rich. She has not told anyone.

Crane found the discrepancy, drew the wrong conclusion — simple greed rather than operational expenditure — and told Maren on the second day of the calm that she had twenty-four hours to explain it to the crew or he would. She spent twenty-four hours deciding how to explain it. She had not decided by the end.

She did not kill Crane. She needed him alive. She understood the moment she saw his body floating off the bow that whoever killed him has arranged things so that her silence is the most plausible explanation for a death she did not cause.

She is now the worst-positioned person on the ship. Guilty of the financial crime. Innocent of the murder. Unable to defend herself without admitting the crime.

---

## Devereux

Jean-Pierre Devereux is fifty-one, La Sierpe's boatswain, and has sailed alongside Crane for four years. Crane told him about the discrepancy on the first day of the calm. He told him he had confronted Maren. He told him to watch and wait.

Devereux has been watching Maren with an intensity that three crew members have noticed and two have commented on. He was the first person on deck the morning Crane was found, though his account — he could not sleep, came up for air before first light — is technically consistent with innocence.

He is frightened, which reads as guilt. He is frightened because he knows what Crane knew and cannot produce the ledger as evidence because he does not know where it is.

The path through Devereux is the longest in the case and the most emotionally resonant. It ends not at his guilt but at his knowledge — he saw the ship's surgeon coming up from below decks at two hours past midnight on the third night of the calm, moving quietly toward the bow, carrying something narrow and wrapped in cloth.

He has been refusing to conclude what this means since the moment Crane's body was found.

---

## Aldric

The surgeon's name is Aldric Noë. He is thirty-four, Dutch-born, and has been La Sierpe's surgeon for fourteen months — joining the crew in Nassau after the previous surgeon died of the same fever that took the previous captain, though the timing of his availability was more convenient than coincidental.

Aldric is the third person who knew about the ledger. He knew because he was the one skimming it. Not Maren — Aldric. Fourteen months in the prize accounting chain, adjusting cargo valuations by small amounts, consistently, in a way only visible when comparing raw cargo assessments to final distribution records. Crane compared them. Four months ago.

Aldric knew Crane was building the ledger because he found him in the cargo hold three months ago conducting an inventory that had no operational purpose. He spent three months watching. By the second day of the calm, when Crane confronted Maren, Aldric understood that Crane had attributed the discrepancy to the wrong person. This gave him a window. If Crane died now, Maren would be the primary suspect, and whoever found the ledger would be looking for evidence of Maren's guilt, not his.

He took a surgical instrument from his own kit — the one used for chest drainage procedures, long enough and thin enough to reach the space between the ribs without leaving a wound a non-physician would immediately identify as deliberate — and he put it in his coat pocket on the third night and waited for the watch rotation gap.

The instrument is in the water. He dropped it off the bow. In the dead calm with no current it has sunk directly below the point it entered the water.

---

## The Ledger's Location

Crane hid the ledger in the most obvious and therefore invisible place on a working ship: behind the prize manifest in the navigator's chart chest, mixed among the official records it was designed to contradict. It has been there for four months.

The navigator — an elderly Portuguese man named Costa — has not opened the secondary compartment in three weeks. The moment someone asks him when he last did, the field collapses. His answer places the ledger's existence in a location that Maren did not plant and Aldric did not search. Only the player, having assembled the full field, has reason to look.

---

## The Three Paths

**Path A — through the financial crime**: Begin with the prize manifests, the distribution discrepancy, the surface case against Maren. This path is coherent and compelling and completely wrong. The ledger, when found, names Aldric's adjustments. The false plateau holds until the ledger's content breaks it.

**Path B — through Devereux's knowledge**: His watchfulness, his early rising, his fear. Converges faster because his observation is the mechanism's direct witness. The player must find a way to make him speak — which requires his grief tokens and his loyalty tokens placed in proximity, which surfaces the enabler: the moment he understood what he saw and chose to be silent.

**Path C — through the surgical instrument's absence**: Begin with the wound. A physician's assessment places the wound's characteristics in a professional context. The surgical kit inventory is a MID phase object token. The empty slot in the kit is LATE phase. Between them they point at the only person on the ship with both the instrument and the anatomical knowledge to use it.

---

## Design notes

La Sierpe is the only case in the set with a fully closed location. The token field cannot reference anything outside the ship — no external witnesses, no distant archives, no documents waiting in cities the ship has not reached. Every token is shipborne: a person, an object in a locker or chest, a time and a location that is still here. The attractor operates under the constraint of the dead calm. This is not a limitation. It is the mechanism's genius.

Maren's guilt is structural. She is guilty of the financial crime and innocent of the murder, which means the player who pursues her correctly identifies the financial fact and incorrectly attributes the killing consequence. The ledger is what breaks the attribution — it names the actual skimmer and redirects `killer_dim` completely. The player does not waste the Maren path. They walk it to the ledger.

The buried motive is the deepest in the set. Aldric Noë is wanted in two jurisdictions under two names for two previous versions of this same crime. Crane's ledger, in its meticulous comparative style, documented not just the La Sierpe discrepancy but a pattern across three vessels and four years, assembled from prize records Crane had been collecting through Nassau and Port Royal contacts — for reasons that had nothing to do with Aldric until they had everything to do with him. Crane was not just the quartermaster. He was the only person on the ship who knew what Aldric actually was.

The dead calm did not trap the killer on the ship. It trapped the only witness.
