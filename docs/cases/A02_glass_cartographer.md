# Adventure Case A02 — The Glass Cartographer
## Central Europe · The Thirty Years War · 1627

| | |
|---|---|
| **Case ID** | `glass_cartographer` |
| **Type** | Adventure (sequential basins, chosen state) |
| **Difficulty** | hard |
| **Basins** | 4 |
| **Protagonist** | Lena Faber, 26, glassmaker's daughter |
| **Final triad** | `ability:glass_reading`, `object:complete_route`, `location:basement_door` |
| **Key branch** | Basin 3: share the route with the Jesuit archivist or find another way through the lines |
| **Full resolution** | The palace basement door opens; what is inside is in the goal state token's surface expression and nowhere else |

*This case is a race. The adventure is against time and convergent threat as much as against puzzles.*

---

The war has been running for nine years and has another twenty-one to go, though nobody knows this yet. What everyone knows is that the roads are dangerous, the armies move unpredictably, and the maps are wrong — deliberately, systematically, commercially wrong. Accurate maps of Central European territory are a military asset in 1627 and the people who possess them do not share them freely.

Lena Faber is twenty-six, the daughter of a glassmaker, and possesses a skill that her father taught her and that she has never seen discussed in any book: she can read the distortions in glass. Old glass, imperfectly made, contains bubbles and striations that encode, if you know how to read them, information about the conditions under which it was made. Her father discovered that a Bohemian glassmaker in the previous century had been encoding navigational information in the glass of church windows across a specific route through Central Europe. The windows are still there. The route leads to something her father spent his life trying to reach and died three miles from.

Three separate parties are converging on her location: an Imperial general, a Swedish intelligence operative, and a Jesuit archivist. She has two weeks before the armies' movements close the roads. She needs to read four more windows, in four different locations, each a day's travel from the last.

---

## Basin One — Leave Before They Arrive

The town of Vrchlabí in eastern Bohemia. Three enabling conditions before she can move: a travel document that will pass military checkpoints, transport, and the glass-reading of the church window she has been working near for two months.

The travel document has three possible solutions: a merchant leaving the region who will add her to his party in exchange for a service; a priest who can issue a different class of document; or a forgery using a seal she can cast in glass — which she can do, which no one knows she can do, and which is both the most elegant solution and the most dangerous if discovered. The horse problem requires navigating the stable master's complicated feelings about Imperial requisitioning. The window reading requires entering the church at dusk — forty minutes when the specific distortions are visible — which requires the sexton's key and a tool from the glazier's workshop that her employer will notice is missing.

When all three conditions are met she leaves on the morning of the third day. The Imperial general's advance scouts arrive that afternoon.

---

## Basin Two — The Monastery At The Pass

The third window is in a monastery in the Krkonoše mountains, occupied not by monks but by a Swedish company using it as a forward observation post. The Swedish intelligence operative — a woman named Britta — is with them, specifically because her intelligence placed the glass route through this window.

The reading requires physical access to the window's exterior face, inside the defensive perimeter. Three branches remain open simultaneously:

1. **Negotiation**: the company's captain has a specific need Lena can meet — a glass lens for surgical extraction of a wound fragment, no physician within range.
2. **Physical circumvention**: a knowledge token about the monastery's original construction, in a document she found in Vrchlabí and has been carrying without reading.
3. **Britta**: if the player has assembled enough of the intelligence operative's token cluster, direct approach becomes possible — Britta's goal and Lena's are not actually opposed, and Britta is intelligent enough to see this if shown correctly.

Each branch closes the others partially. The Britta branch means they are now in a complicated alliance that will reconfigure the rest of the game.

The window at the monastery reveals that the route's destination is not in Bohemia. It is in Venice. The fourth and fifth windows are in the facade of a palace on the Grand Canal. Her father knew this. He died three miles from the Bohemian border because he was going the wrong direction.

---

## Basin Three — Through The Lines

Between Bohemia and Venice in 1627: two armies, three territorial boundaries, one mountain range, the city of Innsbruck under Imperial administration, and four hundred miles of road in varying states of damage.

The Jesuit archivist — aware of Lena's existence since Basin One — surfaces here, not as a threat but as a resource with conditions. He has been tracking the glass route independently for thirty years. He has a travel network that crosses territorial boundaries more reliably than any military document. His condition: she must share what she has read so far.

**The key branch**: sharing unlocks the travel network and makes Basin Three navigable in eight days rather than three weeks. Not sharing means a slower, more dangerous route that permanently closes two character tokens — the merchant contact from Basin One and a border crossing Britta's resources would have enabled.

The Jesuit's knowledge, when shared, adds a layer that recontextualises everything: what Dias encoded in the glass route was not just navigational. It was a proof — a mathematical demonstration, expressed through glass distortion patterns, that a specific navigational problem the Portuguese Crown had been trying to solve for sixty years was unsolvable by the methods they were using. The archive in Venice is not a cache of charts. It is a correction. An argument. A piece of natural philosophy that was too dangerous to publish in 1541 and is still, in 1627, too dangerous to publish without the right institutional protection.

The Jesuit wants that protection to come from Rome. Britta wants it to go to Stockholm. The trading companies want it suppressed. What Lena wants is to understand what her father spent his life looking for and whether it was worth it.

---

## Basin Four — The Palace On The Canal

Venice in 1627 is neutral territory in name and contested in practice. The palace facade contains two windows whose distortions, read in sequence at the correct hour, complete the route and identify the archive's exact location. The palace is occupied by a Venetian family who have lived there for two hundred years and have no idea what is in their windows.

Three enabling conditions: access to the eastern facade (a social problem requiring three CHARACTER tokens from the Venetian contact network); the correct hour for reading (a TIME token constraining the window to a specific tidal condition affecting light through the lagoon water — forty minutes, once per day); and the mathematical key to the final encoding, in the Jesuit's possession, shared only if the player navigated Basin Three in a way that maintained his trust.

Lena reads the glass. She sees the complete route. She knows where the archive is.

It is in the palace's own basement. It has been there since 1588. The Venetian family have been living above it for a hundred and thirty-nine years without knowing it exists.

The final triad: Lena's father's notes, the complete route, and the basement door. The game ends when she opens it.

---

## Design notes

This case runs against time, which no mystery case does. The two-week window before the roads close is encoded in TIME tokens that advance at specific basin completions. The pressure is structural: the player cannot spend unlimited turns in Basin One without losing the window for Basin Two. Urgency is a token class, not a narrative layer.

The three parties converging on Lena encode three different institutional relationships to knowledge: the Imperial general wants maps as military assets; the Swedish operative wants the mathematical proof as diplomatic leverage; the Jesuit wants institutional guardianship. Lena's path through these three is the moral architecture of the game. Who she trusts, who she uses, who she deceives — the field records it all.
