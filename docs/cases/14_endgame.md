---

Arthur Haig is fifty-three years old and has covered chess for the same London newspaper for nineteen years. He is not a chess player himself — he understands the game's structure, its culture, its politics, but he does not play. What he does well, and has always done well, is watch people. He has been watching people play chess in high-pressure conditions since 1953 and he can tell, faster than most, when something beyond the game is being played.

He dies at a diplomatic reception on the evening of September 7th. The hotel doctor records cardiac arrest, consistent with his documented condition and his current medication. His cardiologist in London, informed by telegram the following morning, expresses no surprise. Haig had been managing a difficult heart for eight years.

The cardiac arrest was not managed. It was induced.

---

## Brophy

Dale Brophy is forty-four, a chess correspondent for an American wire service, and a man who has been arguing with Arthur Haig, in print and in person, for six years about the ethics of byline attribution in shared reporting contexts. The argument the evening before Haig's death was the most recent of many. It was witnessed by the reception's coat-check attendant, a Norwegian journalist, and two members of the Icelandic chess federation. Brophy was loud. Haig was quieter and more precise, which was worse.

Brophy is at the diplomatic reception. He has no credible alibi for the twenty minutes around Haig's collapse — he was circulating, speaking to various people, not in anyone's continuous company. He had a documented grievance. He benefits professionally from Haig's absence in a limited sense — the London coverage position will be redistributed.

He did not know that Haig took digoxin twice daily for his arrhythmia. He did not know about the pharmaceutical interaction. He had no access to a compound that would produce it. His motive is a journalist's vanity, not a government's three-day problem.

---

## Vronsky

Nikolai Vronsky is fifty-one, a Soviet team administrator who has been managing chess delegations at international tournaments since 1964. He manages travel arrangements, accommodation, press access, and the various off-board concerns that arise when a chess match becomes a geopolitical event. He is good at it. He is quiet about it. His personnel file, which Haig had not seen but which MI6 had annotated, describes him as the delegation's resident intelligence officer in the same matter-of-fact way that it might describe anyone's secondary function.

He had Haig's medical file. Soviet intelligence had been compiling files on Western correspondents in Reykjavik for three months before the tournament began. Haig's cardiac condition was in the file. His digoxin dosage was in the file. The interaction compound — a vasodilatory agent whose effect on a digoxin patient's heart is documented in Western pharmaceutical literature and presumably Soviet equivalents — was in the delegation's medical bag, listed as a hypertensive treatment for one of the grandmasters.

He put it in the mineral water glass personally. He was standing at the drinks table when Haig arrived at the reception.

---

## Three Days

Haig had been a peripheral MI6 asset for eleven years. He was not an operative. He was a journalist who occasionally passed observations to a handler in London. He passed them through his copy — specific phrasings in specific articles, a simple system that had never been compromised because it had never been taken seriously by anyone looking at it from outside.

In Reykjavik he was looking at something serious.

Grandmaster Petrov was carrying microfilm. This was known within the delegation — not the content, but the fact. Haig had identified the concealment method: Petrov's notation books, in which game analysis was annotated in a hand that was not quite Petrov's and that included position sequences that did not occur in the games being analysed. The books were going to be examined upon return to Moscow. They were not going to make it back to Moscow. Petrov was going to defect publicly, at the closing ceremony, in a manner that would make extraction impossible.

The handler in London knew the plan. The Soviets did not know the plan. They did not know that Haig knew.

Three days before the closing ceremony, Haig died. The books were retrieved from his hotel room two hours later, before the British delegation or Icelandic police had thought to secure it. Petrov played his final games, made no public statement, and returned to Moscow on the delegation's flight.

---

## The Notebook

Haig's personal notebook was found in his hotel room by the British consul's staff officer on the morning after his death. The last entry, dated September 7th, is in a substitution cipher that Haig and his London editor had been using since 1966. The editor decoded it on September 10th. He contacted the consul's office. The consul's office did not pass the communication upward immediately, because the tournament was ending and the administrative situation was complicated.

By the time the information reached the relevant desk in London, Petrov was in Moscow and the notation books were elsewhere.

The notebook is the bridge token. It surfaces through a chain: the hotel room inventory, the British consul staff officer's witness account, the editor's telegram to London, and a reference in MI6 station records to Haig's cipher system. When placed together, these tokens construct a timeline that places Haig's knowledge — and Vronsky's awareness of it — inside the three-day window. The phrase "three days" in the notebook's decoded entry is the token that closes `motive_dim`. Not Haig's death as a punishment for knowing, but Haig's death as a specific operational solution to a specific temporal problem.

## Design notes

The Brophy path is this case's fastest red herring because it is also the loudest. The witnessed argument, the professional grievance, the presence at the reception — these are the things that witnesses remember and that investigators reach for first. The plateau hits when `mechanism_dim` demands pharmaceutical specificity. Brophy's motive does not scale to murder. The compound does not scale to a journalist's grievance.

The redirect comes through the medical access token, which establishes that knowing Haig's specific medication and dosage required either clinical contact or documentation. Haig had not seen a physician in Reykjavik. The only documentation was the intelligence file. The field shifts toward the delegation.

Vronsky is effective because his role is structural. Tournament administrators are everywhere and nowhere — they circulate, they manage, they have access to every room and every conversation as a function of their position. His presence at the drinks table is unremarkable. His knowledge of the compound's interaction is not the kind of knowledge that leaves a visible trace.

The geopolitical layer of this case is deliberately compressed. The player never sees Petrov's notation books. They never learn what was on the microfilm. The motive token carries "defection intelligence" as a category, not a plan, because the specific operational content is above the game's line of sight. What the player assembles is the structure: someone with intelligence access knew that Haig had three days to live before he sent a cable, and made sure the cable was never sent. The case closes on that structure.

The chess itself is background. It is the venue, the cover, the reason six hundred journalists and forty intelligence officers are in the same Icelandic city in September. The game being played in the tournament rooms is not the game this case is about.
