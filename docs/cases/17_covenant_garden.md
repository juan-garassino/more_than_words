Abbot Clement arrives at the monastery on the third day of June. He has come from Winchester. He has not been to Yorkshire before. He is fifty-two, efficient, carrying a letter from the Bishop that grants him full authority to conduct a religious inventory of the house's holdings. He is courteous to the brothers. He does not eat much. He takes a tisane each morning for a heart irregularity that has troubled him since the previous winter.

He has been at the monastery for nine days when he is found in his cell at Lauds, seated at his writing table, the morning tisane half drunk beside him. The Prior sends for the infirmarian. The infirmarian, Brother Anselm, arrives in four minutes. He confirms death. He notes the heart. He says nothing else.

---

## The Plague Summer

The Black Death reached the West Riding in May. Two monks have already died; three more are in the infirmary. The village below the hill has lost one family and fears it will lose more. The monastery's infirmary has been treating villagers as well as brothers, a practice the Abbot had noted with some reservation — resources, he had said, must be husbanded; a larger house in York was better placed to serve the district.

Hargreave arrived ten days before the Abbot, his pack train turned back at two successive market towns. He has eight men with him and an apprentice named Will, seventeen, who was already feverish when they reached the monastery gate. The Prior allowed them in. The Abbot arrived four days later, reviewed the situation, and ruled that the monastery's medical resources could not be expanded to include Hargreave's party. Will Hargreave died on the third of June, the same afternoon the Abbot took up his writing chest in the guest quarters.

Hargreave said, loudly, in the refectory, that the Abbot had killed his boy with a policy. Three brothers heard it. The Prior heard it and said nothing. Brother Anselm, in the corridor, heard it and understood immediately that it would be remembered.

---

## Brother Anselm

He is sixty-seven years old. He has been the monastery's infirmarian and librarian for thirty-one years, a division of duties unusual in Benedictine houses but not unknown. The library and the infirmary share a wing, and in this house the knowledge that serves one has always served the other.

He began translating Arabic medical texts in 1309. He has been at it for forty years. He has no Arabic when he begins — he learns it from the texts themselves, with the help of a correspondence with a Jewish physician in York that lasts six years and then stops. His translations are careful, annotated, technically precise. He has completed thirty-one of the forty-seven texts in the collection. The remaining sixteen he expects to finish before he dies, if he is given the time.

He reads the Abbot's letter to the Bishop of Winchester on the evening of June 8th.

He does not read it through the seal. The Abbot, efficient and unguarded in the way of men who believe their authority is sufficient protection, leaves his writing chest unlocked on the afternoon he visits the infirmary to review the sick brothers' care. Brother Anselm is alone in the guest quarters for eleven minutes. He reads the letter. He replaces it as he found it.

The letter names the collection. It names it with precision — the Abbot has had two days in the library and has made a careful list. Near the end he writes: *materials of doubtful orthodoxy requiring destruction; I will provide a full inventory on return.* He names six titles. All six are among the texts Brother Anselm has translated. Three of them are his most significant work.

## The Foxglove Tisane

The Abbot has been taking a digitalis preparation for his heart each morning since January. The preparation is specified in notes he brought from Winchester — dosage, concentration, infusion time. These notes were given to Brother Anselm on the first morning, as a courtesy, so that the infirmarian could maintain the supply.

The infirmary herb garden has grown foxglove for a hundred years. Brother Anselm has prepared digitalis preparations for three previous patients with heart irregularities. He knows the dosage response with a precision that comes from four decades of infirmary practice and thirty-one translated Arabic medical texts. He knows how much more than the therapeutic dose constitutes a lethal one. He knows that at the Abbot's age and weight, and given the concentration the Winchester notes specify, doubling the preparation and adding a second brewing will produce cardiac arrest within two to four hours and leave no trace that a physician in 1349 would recognise as other than the heart condition itself.

He makes the preparation on the evening of June 11th. He sets it with the morning tisanes. He is the only person who touches those preparations. This is not unusual. It is his infirmary.

## The Merchant

Hargreave is not subtle about his grief or his anger. He does not try to be. His apprentice is dead and he believes the Abbot's policy killed him, which is not entirely wrong — Will might have survived with more aggressive treatment, or he might not have. The plague is uncertain. Hargreave has no way to know and knows he has no way to know, and this uncertainty has made his grief louder than it might otherwise be.

He was seen near the guest quarters on the evening of June 11th. He was there because the guest quarters share a courtyard with the guest stabling, and Hargreave had gone to check his horses. He was not near the Abbot's cell. He did not have access to the infirmary. He does not know the dosage of the Abbot's preparation. He does not know what digitalis is. He is a wool merchant from the West Riding who has lost a boy and is waiting for the roads to open.

He is the most visible figure in the case because his grief performs. He weeps. He argues. He paces the courtyard at night. Brother Anselm, by contrast, is quiet and competent and present only where his duties require him.

The `mechanism_dim` resolves through the infirmary records — the dosage ledger that Brother Anselm keeps, the entry for the Abbot's preparation written in his careful hand, the separate entry for the batch prepared on the 11th that is recorded at double the therapeutic concentration and attributed to an error in measuring that Brother Anselm notes and corrects in the following entry. The error entry is real. The correction entry is fictional. He wrote both.

## The Letter

The Abbot's letter is a LATE phase OBJECT token. Its surface expression is: *a sealed letter in a writing chest, addressed to the Bishop of Winchester.* It is accessible only after a sequence of three MID phase tokens has been placed: the writing chest itself, the fact that the chest was left unlocked, and a witness token establishing Brother Anselm's presence in the guest quarters on the afternoon of June 8th.

When the letter surfaces, it names the Arabic titles. Brother Anselm's late phase token — the translation ledger in the library, in which his work is catalogued with dates and annotations — identifies those same titles by their Arabic names alongside his own Latin renderings. The correspondence is exact. The Abbot listed what Brother Anselm spent his life building.

Before the letter surfaces, the case has two plausible directions: Hargreave, with his visible grief, and Brother Anselm, with his medical access. Most players hold both open until turn eleven. The letter closes one of them. After it surfaces, `motive_dim` moves from partial (infirmary closure, thirty years of work threatened) to complete (forty years of translation, forty-seven texts, the specific titles the Abbot named for destruction). The motive is not the infirmary. The motive is the library. The infirmary is forty years old. The translation is the man's entire life.

## Design notes

The period is the case's distinctive constraint. In 1349, forensic medicine does not exist in any form that could distinguish a carefully prepared foxglove overdose from a cardiac event in a man already known to have a heart condition. The mechanism is undetectable by the investigation available to the case's world. The player assembles it through process evidence — the infirmary records, the preparation log, Brother Anselm's expertise — not through physical trace evidence.

The plague is atmosphere and context, not mechanism. The case's field should include plague tokens that warm the environment (death around the monastery, Hargreave's quarantine, the two dead monks) without those tokens loading onto `mechanism_dim`. Their weight is on the SETTING and WITNESS dimensions. The plague makes Hargreave plausible. It makes death natural-seeming. It does not kill the Abbot.

Brother Anselm is the series' oldest killer. His motive is among the most specific: not the texts in general, not the collection in abstract, but thirty-one completed translations and the certainty that their destruction would leave no copy anywhere. He knows — because he is the only translator, because he knows Arabic through self-teaching, because there is no other copy in England — that if the Abbot's inventory reaches the Bishop, those texts are gone. Four decades of work. Gone because a man from Winchester has decided they are doubtful.

He is not monstrous. He made a decision about proportionality in a world without procedure or appeal. The game names him. What the player feels about it is their own.

The Hargreave red herring is the case's most emotionally available path. Grief is legible. Anger is legible. Scholarly devotion at sixty-seven, expressed as silence and competence and forty years of annotated Latin, requires the player to look more carefully than grief does.
