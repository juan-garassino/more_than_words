---

The printing house for Meşrutiyet occupies the ground floor and basement of a building on the western side of a narrow street in Pera. Its archive is in the basement: three years of correspondence, source materials, documentation assembled over the newspaper's brief life as a voice of the constitutional moment. The archive is what Arslan cares about most. He has been filing materials there for eighteen months.

The fire begins at two in the morning on the fourteenth of July. It starts in the textile warehouse adjacent to the printing house, in the dye chemical storage at the warehouse's rear. The initial response is to the warehouse. By the time anyone understands the fire's direction, the printing house wall has been breached and the archive is burning. The fire is contained before dawn, but the archive is gone.

Mehmed Arslan is found in the basement stairwell. He had gone in to save the letters. He reached the bottom of the stairs.

---

## The Journalist

He was twenty-six. He had worked for Meşrutiyet for three years, from its founding in the early months of the constitutional period. He believed in the newspaper with the particular intensity of young men who find, for the first time, work that matches the shape of their beliefs. He was methodical for a journalist of his age. He kept records.

He had published four significant pieces. The price-fixing piece was his third. It named six merchants — Greek, Armenian, and Ottoman — who had coordinated prices on military supply contracts during the Macedonian operations of 1906 and 1907. The methodology was careful: requisition records, price histories, correspondence. The piece named Petrakis specifically, with documentation.

What Arslan had not published — what he had kept in the archive and told no one about except the contact who obtained it — was a second file. The contact was a clerk at the Greek consulate named Stavros Anagnosti. Anagnosti had come to Arslan in April because Anagnosti had seen things he did not know what to do with, and Arslan had a reputation for knowing what to do with things.

The second file was letters. Twelve of them, spanning three years.

---

## Petrakis

Konstantinos Petrakis is fifty-three and has been a textile merchant in Pera for twenty-one years. He is established, connected, and publicly identified — since Arslan's price-fixing piece — as a man with flexible commercial ethics. He is managing this identification carefully. He has legal counsel. He is not concerned.

He is concerned about the letters.

In 1906, through channels developed over a decade of freight movement along the Salonika–Hamburg route, Petrakis began providing German naval intelligence with photographic copies of Ottoman naval dispositions. Not strategic plans — he did not have access to those — but inventory records, port schedules, fleet composition summaries that moved through the supply chain paperwork his textile routes touched. He passed them through a commercial intermediary in Hamburg. He received payments recorded in his accounts as fabric commissions.

He has been doing this for three years. He has been careful. He did not anticipate that a consulate clerk would be present when a German naval attaché visited the consulate for a discussion that turned, briefly and incautiously, to the subject of their Pera source and his methods.

Anagnosti was in the outer office. He heard what he heard. He spent two months deciding what to do with it before he came to Arslan.

The twelve letters in the archive include Anagnosti's contemporaneous notes, two letters from Anagnosti to Petrakis asking for a meeting (never sent, kept as documentation), and copies of three commercial documents that cross-reference the Hamburg intermediary's name. They do not constitute proof that would survive a court. They constitute enough to make Petrakis a public figure of a different kind, and enough to make the German operation unviable, and enough to destroy him.

Arslan told Anagnosti the letters were in the archive. He said it in the archive, on a Tuesday afternoon. He did not know that the archive room's outer door was not entirely closed, or that Petrakis's purchasing contact in the building trades had come by to discuss a warehouse lease and was waiting in the corridor.

---

## The Warehouse

Petrakis has leased warehouse space adjacent to the printing house for storage of dye chemicals and finished textile stock. He has held the lease for fourteen months — it predates Arslan's price-fixing piece, and predates the letters, and is not suspicious on its face. He knows the warehouse. He knows the dye chemical storage. He has been in that basement twice and understands the combustion behaviour of the chemicals stored there.

Between the tenth and thirteenth of July, Petrakis's warehouse manager — acting on instructions he understood as inventory reorganisation — repositioned the dye chemical drums along the shared wall with the printing house. The repositioning also opened ventilation channels between the warehouse and the printing house's basement stairwell. The warehouse manager is a man named Dimos, who followed his instructions and will say, if asked, that he was doing inventory work.

The fire, once started in the dye chemical storage, followed the geometry that the repositioning created. It was not a fast fire or a hot fire. It was a directed fire. The investigation concludes that the dye chemicals, improperly stored, ignited through summer heat and proximity to an electrical fitting. The investigation does not speak to Dimos or examine the repositioning records.

---

## Officer Salib

Mustafa Salib is a junior officer in the Ottoman provisioning service. His unit processed the supply contracts that Arslan's price-fixing piece used as its primary documentation. The piece made the unit's requisition records public in enough detail to raise questions about oversight. Those questions have not yet become formal, but Salib understands they might, and he understands that the easiest explanation available to his superiors would be to identify the unit's own oversight failures rather than the merchants' coordination.

He sent a letter to Meşrutiyet on the first of July. It is not subtle. It says the newspaper should correct its errors before errors are corrected for it. It is signed with his name and unit, which is either confidence or imprudence or both. The letter is dated two weeks before the fire.

Salib's unit was deployed to a railway construction security assignment outside the city from the seventh to the fifteenth of July. He was not in Pera during the days when the warehouse repositioning occurred or on the night of the fire. His commanding officer has a deployment record. The record is accurate and not in dispute.

He is the most visible figure in the case's early field because his threat is documented and dated and signed. He is also the figure who most clearly could not have done the physical work that the mechanism required. The `mechanism_dim` resolves against him before turn eight for most players. What sustains him longer is that his threat is so explicit — a man who threatens in writing before a fire is either brazen or innocent, and the field does not answer which until the warehouse repositioning token surfaces.

## The Safety Deposit Box

Arslan opened a safety deposit box at the Ottoman Bank in the second week of May. This is a CONTEXT token at EARLY phase — available as a detail of Arslan's financial records, which are part of any investigation of his affairs. The box number is in his personal notebook. The notebook is in his apartment. The apartment is accessible early.

The box requires two things to open: the box number and a letter of authorisation from the account holder, or, in the case of a deceased account holder, a court order. The court order is a procedural token — it takes time in the game's logic, which means it surfaces at LATE phase regardless of when the player reaches for it.

When the box is opened, it contains one item: an envelope. The envelope contains Anagnosti's letter to Arslan, dated April 18th, describing what Anagnosti heard at the consulate. The letter names Petrakis. It names the German naval attaché by rank and description. It describes the Hamburg intermediary. It is not signed with a full name — Anagnosti signed it *your friend in the Greek quarter* — but it is written in a hand that can be matched.

This is the case's bridge token. Before it surfaces, the field has Petrakis as a merchant with documented corrupt behaviour and a possible motive (protecting his price-fixing reputation). After it surfaces, the field has Petrakis as an active intelligence asset with a reason to kill that is categorically different in scale from suppressing a commercial scandal. The `motive_dim` cannot complete on the surface motive alone. It completes when the letter establishes what Arslan actually had.

## Design notes

The Pera setting in 1909 is among the richest period contexts in the series. Three months after the Young Turk revolution, the city is in a state of cautious optimism and organised uncertainty — old power structures nominally dissolved, new ones not yet formed, foreign interests watchful. The token field should carry this as atmosphere: Greek, Armenian, and Ottoman commercial networks operating in the same district; foreign consulates and their overlapping intelligence interests; a newspaper that exists because, for a brief moment, such newspapers could. These SETTING tokens are the field's texture. None of them load onto the invariants. All of them make the invariants legible.

The fire as mechanism is the series' most indeterminate physical event — it destroyed the evidence it was intended to destroy, and it killed Arslan only because Arslan went into the archive. Petrakis did not intend to kill Arslan. He intended to burn the letters. Arslan was in the building by chance or by instinct or by devotion to the archive — the LATE phase token that places Arslan in the basement at two in the morning describes him as having been sleeping in the print room after a late editorial session. He smelled the smoke. He went down.

This matters for the token design. The `killer_dim` saturates on Petrakis through the warehouse repositioning and the property records, not through any evidence of intent to harm Arslan personally. The killing is a consequence of the mechanism. This is not morally different from planning it — Petrakis set a fire in a building where a person was sleeping — but it is mechanically different, and the field should reflect that the mechanism token cluster carries the fire's geometry and the repositioning's deliberateness, not a separate intent token for the killing.

Anagnosti is a thread the player must follow carefully. He is not in the printed narrative except by reference. He appears as a WITNESS token through Arslan's correspondence records, then as a named figure through the bank deposit record, then as an identified person through the consulate employee records. He is never questioned in the investigation's official version. He has left Istanbul. His letter is in the box.
