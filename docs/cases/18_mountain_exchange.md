---

The resort at Arosa sits at fourteen hundred metres. In August the mountain paths are dry and the via ferrata routes are maintained by the hotel's alpine staff, who check the fixed wire anchors each Friday morning. Edwin Cross has walked the eastern route every morning since his arrival. He is a creature of schedule. Everyone at the resort knows this about him by the third day.

Cross is fifty-one, a Treasury economist, in Arosa ostensibly as part of a small informal delegation attending private discussions that are not quite a conference and not quite negotiations. The distinction is deliberate. Nothing here is official. Nothing here will appear in any record except the hotel register.

On the eleventh morning of Cross's stay, the wire on the second pitch of the eastern route — a short traverse above a thirty-metre drop to a boulder field — fails during his crossing. The failure is clean. The wire holds through the first half of the traverse and fails abruptly at the second anchor point. Cross falls eleven metres before a rock ledge arrests him. He is dead before the guides reach him.

The wire at the failed anchor point is a different gauge than the wire across the rest of the route. It is the same diameter. It is not the same tensile strength. Under load it behaves identically to the correct wire until it does not.

---

## Attaché Brand

Klaus Brand is thirty-four. He holds a junior position in the German diplomatic delegation, below the level where the discussions require his presence. He attends meals, manages correspondence, and is visible at the resort's public spaces at conventional hours. His background is in structural engineering — he completed three years at the Technische Hochschule in Berlin before entering diplomatic service. His file, which exists in a basement in Berlin, lists a secondary appointment that does not appear in the delegation's official roster.

He requested a meeting with the alpine maintenance staff on the morning of the ninth day, presenting himself as a guest interested in the technical aspects of the fixed route infrastructure. He spent forty minutes in the equipment room. He signed the guest register. His signature is there.

Brand does not know Cross personally. He had never seen Cross before Arosa. He was told what Cross represented — not what Cross knew, or claimed to know, or could be made to testify to — but what his survival would cost, specifically, within a specific window. He was given that information by a man who arrived at the resort on the seventh day and left on the eighth, and who appears in the hotel register under a name that cannot be verified.

---

## The Heliograph Operation

The Soviet intelligence service has been running an agent in British government since 1932. The agent's cover designation is Heliograph. Cross does not know the agent's name. He knows two things: a code initial, which was passed to him informally and in confidence by a retired Foreign Office colleague who has since died, and a date — the date on which a communication from Heliograph first appeared in a document cross-reference that Cross encountered during a routine departmental review.

These two facts, taken individually, mean nothing. Taken together — by someone who already knew Heliograph's partial identity — they are enough to confirm it.

The Germans identified Heliograph in the spring of 1938 through their own penetration of a Soviet communications network. They spent six months verifying the identification without acting on it. Heliograph, cultivated carefully, is more valuable intact than exposed. The Germans are content to let Heliograph continue, drawing product from the British side to the Soviet side while the Germans read both directions through their access to the Soviet channel. This arrangement has a name in the relevant file but the case does not need it.

Cross was not supposed to know what he knew. The channels that produced his fragments of information were supposed to be secure. They were not. When his visit to Arosa was noted, and his schedule reviewed, and the nature of what he might testify to was assessed, the window was eight days. After eight days he would return to London, and British intelligence would make its own move, and Heliograph would be gone.

Brand was the instrument. The mountain was the opportunity. The schedule was known because Cross had the same schedule every morning and had had it for ten days.

## Novak

Pavel Novak has been a commercial attaché for seven years. He is meticulous, correct, and working in the ordinary course of his duties — commercial treaty discussions that have nothing to do with the military guarantee that Britain has or has not committed to and that will or will not mean anything in six months when the German demands come. He knows the guarantee discussions are happening. He is not part of them. He has been told, quietly, that he is not part of them.

His argument with Cross at dinner on the ninth evening was about exactly that. Cross had made, in the context of a broader conversation about European stability, a remark that struck Novak as indicating British exhaustion with the guarantee's obligations. Cross's remark was analytical, not political. Novak heard it as political. He pressed Cross. Cross, tired and unused to being pressed, said something sharp. Novak said something sharper. Six people at the table witnessed it. Three of them remember it well.

Novak returned to his room at ten that evening and wrote a letter to Prague that he did not send. He burned it in the grate, which his room attendant noticed but did not record until questioned.

He had been in Arosa for two days when Brand replaced the wire. The maintenance equipment room requires a key held by the alpine staff. Novak had no access and no knowledge of the route infrastructure. He had never climbed a via ferrata in his life. He is a commercial attaché from Prague with wire-rimmed glasses and a grief he has been carrying since March.

## The Notebook

Cross carries a small leather notebook he uses for personal memoranda — half professional, half private. He keeps it in the room safe. The safe combination is held by the hotel concierge, as is standard practice for the diplomatic delegation's rooms. The concierge is an OBJECT token at EARLY phase — a detail of the room's arrangements that surfaces naturally in the investigation of Cross's quarters.

The safe itself is a MID phase OBJECT token. Its combination is a WITNESS token, available through the concierge after two prior tokens have established the relevance of the safe's contents.

The notebook contains seven pages of notes from Cross's journey to Arosa, mostly economic. On page four there is a passage in shorthand — Cross uses Pitman, which his secretary has never learned. The shorthand reads, in full: *H— initial only, confirm via R. date 14 Feb 32. If asked directly: no.* Below this, underscored: *Heliograph.*

This is a LATE phase SECRET token. Its surface expression is *a notation in shorthand, page four of the notebook.* It requires a decoder token — a witness who knows Cross used Pitman, available through a colleague token at the resort — to become legible. Once legible, it connects to a Soviet intelligence CONTEXT token that has been circulating in the field since the mid-game: an abstract reference to a British double agent that surfaces through the diplomatic archive tokens but does not name or confirm Heliograph until the notebook entry does.

When the notebook token, the decoder token, and the Soviet intelligence context token are placed together, `motive_dim` shifts from the surface reading — Cross was carrying political intelligence about Czechoslovakia — to the buried reading: Cross was the only living corroborating source for the identity of a Soviet agent that the Germans had spent six years cultivating and had no intention of losing.

## Design notes

This case has two overlapping institutional frames — German intelligence protecting a cultivated Soviet double agent, British Treasury economics intersecting with signals intelligence — and the player must assemble both before the buried motive becomes visible. Most players arrive at the surface motive (political intelligence, Czechoslovakia guarantee) by turn eight. The buried motive requires the Heliograph thread and cannot surface before the notebook is placed.

The `mechanism_dim` is the case's clearest differentiator. The wire substitution requires structural engineering knowledge — specifically the ability to identify a wire that will pass visual inspection at the correct diameter while failing at load because its tensile strength specification is lower. This knowledge is specific and documented: Brand's Technische Hochschule record, which surfaces as a MID phase WITNESS token through the delegation's formal credentials, is the moment the mechanism path consolidates.

Novak is the most emotionally coherent red herring in the series for this position. His country's existence is at stake. His argument with Cross was real. His distress is documented by multiple witnesses. The player who follows him is following a thread that is nationally significant, personally genuine, and mechanically impossible. The mechanism breaks it cleanly: two days at the resort, no access to the infrastructure, no relevant knowledge. The redirect from Novak to Brand happens through the maintenance equipment room — the signed register is a MID phase OBJECT token that places Brand in the right room on the right day.

The absence of a direct connection between Brand and Cross until the buried motive surfaces is deliberate. They never spoke. They were never introduced. Brand's operation was surgical and impersonal. The case's field should reflect this: the Brand token cluster warms slowly through indirect evidence (the signed register, the structural engineering record, the unverifiable name in the hotel register) before the `killer_dim` consolidates. This is harder than cases where the killer and victim had a prior relationship. The player must build the connection inferentially.
