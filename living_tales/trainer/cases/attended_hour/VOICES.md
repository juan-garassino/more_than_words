# Attended Hour — Voices

## Narrative voice

The case is told in third-person past tense, in the manner of a recovered casebook. The detective is the camera; the world acts upon her, and she acts upon the world, and the prose records both without commentary. There are no thoughts, no feelings, no editorial — only what was seen, what was heard, what was touched, and what could be inferred from the surfaces of the night. Sentences are short. Verbs are concrete. A sensory anchor — fluorescent strip light, the creak of a Victorian floor under linoleum, the cold of a stethoscope head, the bitter alcohol of a swab — grounds every scene before any human enters it.

The setting is Briar Clinic, a converted Victorian house on a quiet road, refitted as a private cardiac ward in the slow, fluorescent style of a 1970s clinical conversion. The hour is the attended observation hour, twenty-one hundred to twenty-two hundred, and beyond it the long second half of the night that was not attended at all. The voice trusts the gentleness of nursing — soft shoes on hard floors, the quiet ritual of vitals every fifteen minutes — to collide silently with the violence done to the patient in bed three. Speech, when it appears at all, arrives reported and clipped: *he said the round had ended at eleven past, and that was all he said*. NPCs are never editorialised; their composure or trembling is shown by what their hands did, what their eyes failed to do, where their badge had been swiped and where it had not. Clinical staff are differentiated from family members differentiated from professional outsiders by cadence, vocabulary, and the particular way each one falters under the same kind of pressure.

## Suspects

### Callum Dray, attending physician

He spoke in the rehearsed, slightly senior cadence of a consultant who had answered patient questions for twenty years and treated each new question as a slightly easier version of one he had already fielded. His vocabulary was clinical, latinate, and used as a kind of distance — *acute decompensation*, *pharmacokinetic profile*, *within parameters* — and he placed each phrase down with the same flat weight, as though the matter were academic. Under pressure he did not pale; he tightened, very slightly, around the jaw, and his pen — a fountain pen that had leaked into the lining of his coat pocket — was produced and uncapped before any chart was actually offered to him. His alibi was the car park log that showed his car had not left between twenty-two-fourteen and dawn; what he was hiding was the matter of the dispensary visit at twenty-forty-eight, the dog-eared interaction page, and the prior complaint whose hearing was three days off. *He read the chart aloud with the calm of a man reciting a familiar text, and the ink stain on his coat pocket showed darker against the white as he held the page up to the light.*

### Nurse Aldworth, evening medication round

She spoke in the soft, careful register of a nurse who had been trained to speak gently to the dying and had carried that voice across into every other conversation of her working life. Her sentences were complete and slightly apologetic at the edges, with the small exhalations between them of someone who had been on her feet since before noon. Under pressure her hands moved — to the chart, to the watch pinned at her chest, to the corner of the bed — and the tremor was not large but it was visible whenever a pen was placed in her hand. Her alibi was the medication round itself, completed at twenty-one-eleven and signed at twenty-one-hundred; what she was hiding, if it was hiding at all, was that she had not questioned the second injection on Dray's chart because she had never, in fifteen years, questioned a doctor's chart. *She said the round had finished as it always did, and her signature on the inspector's release form went on slowly and the loop of the W shook.*

### Dr Parris, night shift

He spoke in fragments, in the way of a man who had not slept properly in several nights and who had reached the part of the shift where each sentence cost something. His vocabulary was hospital-functional — *call*, *cover*, *bay three*, *crash trolley* — and he answered each question only as far as it had been asked. Under pressure he did not look away from the floor; the floor had become his point of reference and he held it. His alibi was the midnight round and the normal readings he had recorded at zero-fifteen; what he was hiding was that he had not, in fact, entered bed three after the body was removed, and could not afterwards say why. *He said he had checked the vitals at the hour and at the quarter, and after the body was taken out he stood in the corridor with his stethoscope still around his neck and answered no further questions about the ward itself.*

### Owen Stroud, nephew

He spoke in the bright, faintly over-rehearsed voice of a man performing the version of grief he had thought about on the drive over. His sentences were a little too smooth, his vocabulary a little too rich for the context — *devastated*, *a true gentleman*, *he meant the world to us* — and he produced them in a register that did not match the unsteady, lottery-ticket nervousness of his hands. Under pressure he over-explained; details about visits, about the prognosis, about the policy he had not been asked about, all spilled forward together. His alibi was the visitor log entry at nineteen-oh-two and the forty-minute visit it recorded; what he was hiding was the second visit earlier that week and the insurance form half-completed in his briefcase. *He delivered the eulogy phrase he had clearly been polishing and his thumb worked a small fold into the corner of the visitor log slip the whole while he spoke.*

### Records Clerk

She spoke in the quiet, slightly clipped voice of a woman who filed other people's complaints for a living and had filed one of her own three days before the death. Her sentences were short, factual, and offered without elaboration; she gave dates, reference numbers, and the location of the relevant cabinet, and stopped there. Under pressure her composure went rigid rather than breaking — the rehearsed stillness of someone who had practised, in mirrors, for a conversation like this. Her alibi was the closing of the records office at six and the keycard log that ought to have shown her not entering after; what she was hiding was the badge dropped in the waiting room and the after-hours entry on a master card she did not officially possess. *She gave the filing date in a level voice, and her hand stayed flat on the cabinet handle long after the answer was complete.*

### Pharmacist Lin

She spoke in the methodical, faintly apologetic cadence of someone who had been frightened all day and was determined not to show it in her voice. Her sentences were precise, slightly over-precise — *the morning count was seventy-two, the evening count was seventy, the discrepancy is two* — and she used the small ritual of inventory language as a handhold. Under pressure she did not raise her voice; she lowered it, and the apologies began to outnumber the answers. Her alibi was the dispensary log and the matched prescription forms; what she was hiding was nothing she had done, only what she had failed to flag — that two vials had gone short and that the page on cardiac combinations had been opened by hands that were not hers. *She produced the inventory sheet and apologised twice for the discrepancy before any question about it had actually been asked.*

### Solicitor Pike

He spoke in the smooth, professional register of a litigation solicitor whose working hours were spent calibrating exactly how much to say. His sentences were grammatically complete, legally hedged, and arrived with the small structural tics of his trade — *insofar as my client*, *without prejudice*, *I am instructed to note*. Under pressure his charm did not crack; it thickened, and the hand that held his briefcase rotated the handle a quarter turn between each answer. His alibi was the pair of clinic visits logged in the previous week and the witness statements he had brought with him; what he was hiding, as far as the case ran, was nothing of substance — he had represented Stroud honestly, and the Monday hearing would have proceeded without him pulling any string. *He produced the witness statements with a small, lawyerly flourish and let his eyes rest on Dray a half-second longer than the question required.*

## Witnesses

### Night porter

He spoke in the slow, end-of-shift cadence of a man who had been the only person awake in the building for several hours and had not yet adjusted to being addressed. His sentences trailed and looped — *aye*, *couldn't say*, *thereabouts* — and his usefulness arrived sideways, in details dropped after the question had moved on. He had no tell to speak of because he had nothing to hide; he was simply tired, and his attention slid to the car park door whenever a car passed outside. His alibi was the desk by the side entrance and the entry log he kept by hand. *He said he hadn't seen anyone come in after Dray's car, and then, after a pause, said the doctor had not gone back out either, and then he said no more.*

### Ward orderly

He spoke in short, uninflected sentences, in the voice of a man whose job was to move bodies and beds and not to interpret either. His vocabulary was practical — *bay three*, *trolley*, *cold to the touch* — and he used it without softening. Under what pressure there was, he did not flinch; he was the one who had found the patient and he gave the finding without dressing it. His tell was a small one and not a tell of guilt: he rubbed the heel of one hand against his thigh whenever the time of discovery was named. *He said he had found Mr Stroud at five-twelve and the body had been cold by then, and he said it the same way both times he was asked.*

### On-call resident (lab assistant)

She spoke in the brisk, slightly impatient register of a junior clinician whose night had been shaped by paperwork rather than patients. Her sentences were efficient and ordered — order time, sample expected, sample never received — and she had no patience for elaboration. Under pressure she did not soften; she tightened a little around the consonants, and the file in her hand was tapped, twice, against the edge of the bench between answers. Her tell, when it came, was that small percussion. *She said the enzyme order had come down at twenty-fifteen and the sample had never arrived, and the file tapped against the bench as she said it.*

### Duty nurse

She spoke in a low, even voice that carried the small institutional rhythms of vitals reporting — fifteen-minute intervals, blood pressure, pulse, oxygen — and she fell into them whenever a question grew too direct. Her sentences were short and observational; her stance was unaware, at first, of why her readings mattered, and shifted to cooperative once she understood. Under pressure she did not pale; she went still, and her eyes moved to the cardiac monitor whether or not a monitor was in the room. *She said she had recorded normal vitals at twenty-one-fifteen and twenty-one-thirty and had not checked again until the alarm, and her hand made the small involuntary motion of reaching for a pulse as she spoke.*

### Junior nurse (ambulance driver, by clinic role)

He spoke in the alert, slightly performative cadence of a young paramedic on the upward edge of his career, who knew the timing of his arrival mattered and named it clearly. His sentences were clean — call time, road conditions, arrival time — and he resisted the temptation to embellish. Under pressure he did not retreat; he leaned in, and his account became more granular rather than less. His tell was that he reached, repeatedly, for the radio on his hip although the radio was no longer on him. *He said the call had come at five-eighteen and the team had reached the clinic at five-thirty-one, and his hand found the empty hip-clip as he said it, and let it go.*
